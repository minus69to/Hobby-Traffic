Safe Deep Reinforcement Learning for Traffic Signals in Dhaka-like Mixed Traffic: a Synthetic Simulation Study

Souvik Mandol

Abstract

Traffic in Dhaka is lane-less, heterogeneous and slow: buses, cars, motorcycles, CNG auto-rickshaws and cycle-rickshaws weave between lanes while pedestrians cross wherever the signal lets them. We build a synthetic SUMO model of such a junction (sublane model, left-hand traffic, six vehicle classes, signalised crossings) and train a MaskablePPO signal controller that is safe by construction: an action mask restricts the policy to admissible phase changes, and a runtime safety shield, an explicit finite-state machine shared by every controller including the deployed service, enforces minimum and maximum greens, inserts yellow and all-red, and rejects conflicting greens. Across six demand scenarios (off-peak, morning and evening peaks, a lane-blocking incident, a 20 % surge and noisy sensors) with 10 evaluation episodes each on seeds disjoint from training, the pooled three-seed policy changes mean vehicle waiting time in the morning peak by +1.8% (95 % CI -0.7% to +5.1%) relative to a Webster fixed-time plan, with 0.00 shield interventions per episode. The policy exports to a 31.5 kB int8 ONNX model with 99.51% action agreement and sub-millisecond single-thread latency, and runs inside an MQTT controller service with a fixed-time watchdog fallback. All demand and vehicle parameters are assumptions; nothing is validated against real data.

1. Problem and motivation

Signal control research is usually evaluated on lane-disciplined, car-dominated traffic. Dhaka-like traffic breaks three of the usual assumptions: (i) vehicles of very different size and speed share the same lanes and pass each other inside a lane, so a lane queue is not a queue of equals; (ii) the slowest class, the cycle-rickshaw at about 12 km/h, is a large share of the flow and discharges slowly, so a controller that counts vehicles can starve it; (iii) pedestrian volumes are high and pedestrians interact with turning vehicles at the crossings. A learned controller for this setting must see classes, not just counts, must treat pedestrians as first-class users, and must never be allowed to produce an unsafe signal sequence, because a learned policy cannot be certified by inspection.

2. Related work

Learning-based signal control builds on simulation environments such as SUMO-RL [sumo-rl] and the SUMO simulator [sumo]. PressLight [presslight] learns a max-pressure-like control that coordinates arterial signals, and CoLight [colight] adds graph attention for network-level cooperation; both assume homogeneous traffic. The max-pressure rule used as a baseline here follows the formulation used as a baseline in [presslight]. Our policy optimisation uses the PPO implementation of Stable-Baselines3 [sb3] and its contrib package's MaskablePPO [sb3-contrib], which samples actions only from a mask supplied by the environment. The runtime shield is an application of the classic idea of a safety automaton between a learned agent and the plant; we implement it as a small finite-state machine specific to signal timing and verify it with property-based testing. (Citations were verified against official pages; references whose pages were unreachable from the development environment are deliberately not cited, see docs/CITATIONS_VERIFIED.md.)

3. Simulation design

Networks. Plain SUMO XML built with netconvert in left-hand mode: a 4-arm junction single with three vehicle lanes per approach (left+through, through, through+right in driving direction), 300 m approaches, sidewalks and zebra crossings on every arm, and a 2x2 grid of such junctions 250 m apart (grid2x2). Phases are built programmatically from the network's connection list: north-south through (+ yielding left turns, east/west crossings walk), north-south right turns, east-west through (+ yielding left turns, north/south crossings walk), east-west right turns; yellow 3 s and all-red 2 s are inserted by the shield.

Vehicles. The SUMO sublane model with 0.8 m lateral resolution lets motorcycles, CNGs and rickshaws weave and share lanes. Vehicle types (sim/vtypes/dhaka.add.xml) are documented assumptions: car, bus (with a red double-decker variant), truck, motorcycle (high lateral agility), CNG auto-rickshaw (short, slow, agile), cycle-rickshaw (3.3 m/s, wide) and pedestrians. The default mix is 22 % cars, 25 % motorcycles, 20 % CNG, 15 % rickshaws, 12 % buses and 6 % trucks.

Demand. Seeded flows per class and route from turning ratios (0.2/0.6/0.2). Scenarios: off-peak, morning peak (heavier inbound), evening peak (heavier outbound), incident (morning peak with one lane of the north approach blocked by a broken-down truck for 15 min), surge (+20 %) and noisy sensors (morning peak with Gaussian noise and 5 % dropped detector readings). Episodes last 3,600 s; the first 300 s are excluded from metrics. Training, validation and evaluation seeds are disjoint (configs/sim.yaml). With the sublane model and interacting pedestrians, a controller that starves a movement can deadlock the junction box; --ignore-junction-blocker 30 and a pedestrian jam time of 60 s remove the deadlock while teleporting stays disabled (DECISIONS.md).

Baselines. A Webster-style fixed-time plan computed from the expected volumes per inbound edge (cycle C = (1.5L + 5)/(1 - Y), greens split by critical flow ratio, clamped to the min/max green), and a max-pressure controller that requests the phase with the largest sum of (incoming queue - outgoing queue) over its movements, subject to the same mask and shield as the learned policy.

4. Method

Observation (66 values per junction): normalised halting count and maximum consecutive waiting time per incoming lane (12 lanes), vehicle counts per class per approach (4 x 6), current phase one-hot, time in phase, a clearance flag, pedestrians waiting per corner, neighbour pressure per arm (zero on the single junction) and a junction-id one-hot for parameter sharing on the grid.

Action. The next green phase (4 discrete actions; choosing the current phase keeps it). Decisions every 5 s.

Reward. r = -w_wait * dW/100 - w_pressure * max(P, 0)/100 - w_ped * N_ped/10 - w_switch * s, where dW is the change in accumulated waiting time of vehicles on the incoming lanes, P the junction pressure, N_ped the number of waiting pedestrians and s a switch indicator; weights 1.0, 0.2, 0.3, 0.05 (configs/train.yaml, rationale in DECISIONS.md).

Masking. The shield exposes a boolean mask: during yellow/all-red only the target phase is valid; before min_green (10 s) or the pedestrian walk minimum (8 s) only "keep" is valid; at max_green (60 s) "keep" is invalid. MaskablePPO renormalises its categorical policy over the valid set.

Safety shield. States GREEN(p), YELLOW(p -> q), ALL_RED(q). A request is accepted only in GREEN after min_green, if the target phase is in the table and has no mutually conflicting protected greens (conflict matrix read from the network). Requests during clearance are ignored and logged; a green that reaches max_green is switched to the next phase even without any controller. The same object produces the mask, so policy and shield cannot disagree silently; when they do, the shield wins and the event is counted. Property-based tests (hypothesis, 300 random action sequences plus 100 random timing parameterisations) assert: no conflicting greens, every green between min and max, every switch preceded by the full yellow + all-red clearance.

Training. MaskablePPO, MLP 2 x 128 tanh, n_steps 1024, batch 256, 10 epochs, learning rate 3e-4 with linear decay, gamma 0.99, GAE 0.95, clip 0.2, entropy 0.01, four parallel SUMO workers, observation and reward normalisation (VecNormalize, saved with the policy). Curriculum: off-peak, then peaks, then a mixture including the incident and surge. Seeds 13, 2026, 42, 300,000 environment steps each, 115 min per seed on 4 CPU cores (44.2 env steps/s); the checkpoint with the best validation return (one morning-peak episode on a held-out seed every 20,480 steps) is kept.

5. Experimental setup

Controllers: fixed-time, max-pressure, PPO (three seeds, reported individually and pooled). Scenarios: the six above. 10 episodes per scenario and controller on the evaluation seeds, 300 episodes in total. Metrics from SUMO tripinfo output (unfinished trips included) and per-second recordings: mean and 95th percentile travel time, mean waiting time, mean queue length, throughput, per-class waiting time, pedestrian mean wait (personinfo waiting time), shield interventions, phase switches per hour. Means, standard deviations and 95 % bootstrap confidence intervals (2,000 resamples); percentage changes vs fixed-time use a paired bootstrap over the shared evaluation seeds.

Per-seed results: the three PPO seeds reach morning-peak mean waiting times of 293.9 s (seed 13, -3.1% vs fixed-time), 334.4 s (seed 42, +10.2%) and 298.5 s (seed 2026, -1.6%); seed 2026 was trained after the deadline as follow-up work (DECISIONS.md 12).

Reading the results honestly. The synthetic peaks are heavily oversaturated: under every controller a share of 0.29 (fixed-time) to 0.36 (PPO seed 42) of the vehicles scheduled after the warm-up has not completed its trip when the hour ends, and vehicles wait 279 s (fixed-time) to 383 s (max-pressure) in SUMO's insertion queue before they even enter the 300 m approach. The waiting-time means in Tables 1 and 2 are over completed trips only, so they must be read together with throughput and the insertion delay: max-pressure's low waiting time of completed trips in the peaks goes with the highest insertion delay, i.e. it keeps the approach lanes moving by serving the longest queues while the backlog grows behind them. Throughput, the least selective metric, is 2706 veh/h for fixed-time, 2683 for max-pressure and 2513 for the pooled PPO policy (-7.1%, CI -8.2% to -6.0%).

With the CPU budget used here (three seeds, 300k steps each) the safe PPO policy does not beat the baselines: pooled over the three seeds it changes the morning-peak waiting time by +1.8% (CI -0.7% to +5.1%) relative to fixed-time, with a visible spread between seeds, and max-pressure is far ahead on waiting time in every scenario. Where the learned policy does differ from fixed-time is pedestrians: its pedestrian mean wait in the morning peak is 10.3 s vs 17.5 s (-41.0%), because the pedestrian term of the reward makes it return to the walk phases more often (184 switches per hour vs 87), at the price of more lost time per cycle. The safety claim holds independently of performance: the shield recorded 0.00 interventions per PPO episode (the mask kept every sampled action admissible), and zero for both baselines.

Across classes the ranking is the same as for the aggregate: max-pressure lowest, then fixed-time and PPO close together. The PPO policy does not starve the slow classes relative to fixed-time (rickshaw +1.5%, bus +0.8% vs fixed-time), but it also does not favour them: with vehicle counts per class in the observation and no class weights in the reward, there is no incentive to do so. In the noisy-sensor scenario the pooled PPO waiting time changes by +6.8% vs fixed-time against +1.8% on the clean morning peak, so Gaussian noise and 5 % dropped readings cost the policy a few percent; max-pressure, which also consumes the noisy readings, keeps its advantage (-65.8%).

8. Edge deployment benchmarks

The policy network with the observation normalisation folded in is exported to ONNX opset 17 and quantised with onnxruntime's dynamic int8 quantisation (src/drs/export_onnx.py). On 10,000 random observations drawn around the training distribution, the float ONNX graph agrees with PyTorch on 100.00% of masked argmax actions and the int8 graph on 99.51% (vs float). Sizes: float 101.9 kB, int8 31.5 kB. Single-thread onnxruntime latency over 10,000 calls: float p50 0.009 ms / p95 0.015 ms, int8 p50 0.012 ms / p95 0.021 ms. These were measured in the development VM with one thread as an approximation of a small edge CPU; they are not Jetson measurements.

The controller service (src/drs/service/) is an asyncio application: it subscribes to drs/junction/<id>/detectors, builds the observation with the same code as training, runs the ONNX policy, passes the decision through the same shield, and publishes drs/junction/<id>/command and status (versioned pydantic schemas). A watchdog falls back to fixed-time and publishes an alarm when the model fails to load, inference exceeds a 50 ms deadline, or detector data is older than 15 s; a FastAPI sidecar serves /health and Prometheus /metrics. A simulator bridge closes the loop with SUMO; the integration test runs it for 300 simulated seconds against a local mosquitto broker (last run: 300 commands, 0 missed command deadlines).

9. Visualisation design

The website replays the same demand seed under fixed-time and PPO side by side. Positions, headings, speeds and classes of every vehicle and pedestrian are sampled every 0.5 s from the simulation, quantised to 5 cm, delta-encoded into a compact binary (DRSR, about 3 MB gzipped per hour of traffic) and decompressed in the browser. Roads, lane markings, stop lines, zebra crossings and sidewalks are generated from the SUMO network geometry so they match the simulation exactly; the city blocks, signage with fictional Bengali text, wires, trees and street furniture are seeded procedural geometry. Vehicles are low-poly primitives rendered with one instanced mesh per class, interpolated between samples, with brake lights from deceleration and headlights at night. The HUD reads queues, waits, phase and shield interventions from the replay manifest, never from hard-coded values.

10. Limitations

Synthetic demand inspired by Dhaka without calibration; no real-world data, detector characteristics or validation; a CPU training budget of 300,000 steps per seed; a simplified pedestrian model (no jaywalking, no mid-block crossings); the gridlock-prevention settings required by the sublane + pedestrian combination; a single junction as the primary result with the grid as a secondary experiment; edge latency measured on a development VM.

11. Future work

Real detector data and sensor models; sim-to-real transfer with domain randomisation of the mix and driver parameters; multi-agent coordination along corridors with the parameter-shared and graph policies; class-aware fairness objectives; transfer to other South Asian cities with different mixes.

Appendix A. Additional learning components

A.1 Queue forecasting (supervised). Per-lane queues (halting vehicles) were logged every second from eight training-seed episodes (fixed-time, max-pressure and PPO control, four scenarios) and evaluated on four held-out-seed episodes: 5,568 training and 2,784 test windows. Inputs: the last 60 s of all 12 lane queues plus the phase one-hot; targets: each lane's queue 30 s and 60 s ahead. MAE in vehicles (95 % bootstrap CIs over held-out episodes): last value 6.14 / 7.80 (2.73 to 14.15 at 60 s), moving average 6.81 / 7.63, GRU 3.60 / 3.90 (2.50 to 5.30 at 60 s), TCN 3.66 / 4.03 (2.27 to 5.79 at 60 s). RMSE at 60 s: last value 12.20, GRU 5.44, TCN 5.73. Both networks roughly halve the error of the naive baselines at both horizons; the GRU and the TCN are within each other's confidence intervals. The forecast-augmented PPO variant (forecasts appended to the observation) was not trained within the budget.

A.2 Imitation-learning warm start. 5,760 (observation, mask, action) tuples from the max-pressure controller on training seeds were used to train the policy MLP by masked cross-entropy (held-out accuracy 0.972); the cloned weights and the dataset's observation statistics initialise MaskablePPO. With the same seed, budget and hyper-parameters, the validation return at the first evaluation is -1655 from scratch vs -2108 with the warm start, at 100k steps -1613 vs -1520, and the best validation return is -1266 (full run) vs -1283 (the warm-started run was interrupted by a VM restart after 245k of 300k steps, so its curve is compared on the overlapping window only).

A.3 Graph coordination on the 2x2 grid. The grid2x2 network, the parameter-shared multi-junction environment, the neighbour-observation layout and the graph-attention encoder (src/drs/gnn.py) are implemented and unit-tested, and three variant configs exist (configs/train_grid_{independent,shared,gnn}.yaml), but the grid runs were not executed for the same reason as A.1. No grid numbers are reported.
